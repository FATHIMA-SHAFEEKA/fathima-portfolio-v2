01 / The challenge

Visitors can see history.
But can they understand it?

Visitors often encounter historical features without finding enough information to understand how they were made or why they matter. Existing signboards may provide basic facts without answering deeper questions.

“I could see the results of the ancient construction, but I couldn't fully understand the process behind it.” Participant P2 · Research interview

The design challenge

How might we help Sri Lankan family visitors understand a specific heritage feature at the moment they become curious?

The proposed solution

A mobile web guide that allows visitors to scan or select a feature and read a short explanation in Sinhala or English, with additional details when needed.

02 / Research and discovery

Understanding the people
behind the problem.

I used four qualitative interviews about visits to Sri Lankan heritage sites to identify recurring questions, information gaps and language needs.

Affinity mapping — grouping observations into five themes.

Thematic analysis — connecting participant evidence to design opportunities and user stories.

INSIGHT 01

Seeing isn't understanding

All four participants described an unanswered question. Three wanted more explanation about how, why or background.

INSIGHT 02

Signboards have limits

Participants valued basic information, but three described signs that were too brief or unclear.

INSIGHT 03

Language shapes clarity

Language came up in all four interviews, although the questions may have prompted it.

Research limitation: This was a small, directional study of four participants. Only one discussed Sigiriya. The interviews did not establish actual on-site scanning behavior.

06 / Visual direction

Inspired by the place,
not competing with it.

Deep jungle greens, warm ochre accents, soft neutral surfaces and editorial typography create a calm visual identity that keeps the heritage experience at the center.

07 / Final interface design

Turning complexity into
clear, useful screens.

08 / Design system and handoff

Designed for consistency.
Documented for clarity.

Reusable component patterns and defined interaction states make the interface easier to maintain and communicate to developers.

Component specifications — buttons, focus and disabled states, search inputs, feature cards, language controls and messages.

COMPONENTS

Reusable patterns

Consistent buttons, feature cards, app bars, language selectors and message boxes.

INTERACTIONS

Documented states

Default, pressed, focus, disabled, loading, error and unavailable-content behavior.

ACCESSIBILITY

Inclusive decisions

Touch targets of at least 44 px, readable labels and feedback that doesn't depend on color alone.

09 / Reflection and next steps

What I learned.
What comes next.

Key design takeaways

A strong interface starts with understanding what users are trying to accomplish, not with choosing visual styles.

Documenting alternative paths and failure states is as important as designing the successful journey.

Small-sample research should guide hypotheses without being presented as universal proof.

Next validation steps

Test the clickable prototype with Sri Lankan heritage visitors, including Sinhala-first participants.

Measure time to first answer, task ease and whether visitors can explain a feature in their own words.

Validate on-site scanning before treating it as a proven solution.
